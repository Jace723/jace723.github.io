When Do Programmable Vouchers Improve Public Spending?

An evaluation model for rule enforcement, access, and administrative cost

What does the model evaluate?

DefinitionWhen Do Programmable Vouchers Improve Public Spending? is a conceptual working paper by Jace Shim (2026) that develops an evaluation model for deciding when enforcing voucher conditions, such as merchant eligibility and expiry, at checkout improves public spending relative to an existing rule-based voucher. The model tracks whether eligible recipients complete intended purchases, whether ineligible purchases are prevented, and how the intervention shifts costs across agencies, merchants, and households.

Central propositionAn apparent improvement in compliance can conceal a loss of access when a system blocks eligible purchases or leads households to give up before checkout.

Research question

When does enforcing voucher conditions at checkout improve public spending relative to an existing rule-based voucher? A municipal agency might limit a food benefit to designated merchants and a particular period; the paper asks where that rule should be enforced.

Three places to enforce the same rule

At the shop

Checked by merchants

Merchants verify eligibility when the voucher is presented.

After payment

Audited later

Transactions are reviewed after they have been made.

Before settlement

Enforced at checkout

The payment system verifies the condition when the beneficiary attempts payment.

These designs distribute error, delay, and administrative work differently. The relevant baseline is the program’s actual processing arrangement, which may already automate some checks.

The evaluation model

Register three outcomes per 1,000 eligible households in each design over the same observation window.

THouseholds that complete a policy-eligible purchase
DExpected harm of noncompliant spending that succeeds, under a common audit protocol
CTotal implementation and use cost across the agency, merchants, and households
wIncremental social value of reaching an additional eligible household

Change in value = w × ΔT − ΔD − ΔC

Cost includes integration and operation, reconciliation, training, appeal handling, and households’ time and travel; record who incurs each cost and avoid counting pure transfers as resource costs. Specify benefit valuation and what counts as harm before the evaluation, use a fixed time horizon, and run sensitivity analysis for w and for the assumed cost of improperly directed spending.

Distributional constraint

Before deployment, set a minimum acceptable eligible completion rate and a maximum acceptable decline for groups with access barriers. Compare outcomes by merchant density, device access, and need for assisted transactions, and include unsuccessful attempts and beneficiaries who never reach checkout. A favorable aggregate result can coexist with a subgroup breach.

Outdated records and system failures may block authorized purchases. A staffed override or later review can recover such transactions while preserving an auditable decision trail.

Worked example (hypothetical)

Checkout enforcement reduces improper-spending harm and administrative burden by a combined 9,000 units, but 60 fewer households complete eligible purchases, each valued at 200 units.

Per 1,000 eligible households (invented quantities)
Savings: less harm and burden (−ΔD − ΔC)
+9,000
Lost value: 60 completions × 200 (w × ΔT)
−12,000

Change in assessed value: −3,000 · Break-even value: 150 units per lost completion

The quantities are invented. A subgroup access breach can independently trigger a redesign under the predeclared minimum standard.

Three testable propositions

P1

Rule verifiability

The reduction in successful noncompliant purchases should be larger for rules tied to reliable, machine-readable records than for rules requiring context at checkout, conditional on baseline compliance and merchant coverage.

P2

Recoverability and access

A documented assisted path and a time-bounded override should reduce the eligible completion gap associated with checkout enforcement. Test the effect separately where merchant coverage or device access is limited, and track overrides, subsequent authorization, and time to resolution.

P3

Distribution of burden

Automated checks should reduce manual reconciliation when programs can reuse stable payment integrations. In fragmented settings, merchant onboarding, software maintenance, and beneficiary support may offset those savings. Measure total costs and report them separately by actor.

Implementation context

Korea

Project Hangang

The Bank of Korea describes Project Hangang as an experiment combining wholesale central bank digital currency with bank-issued tokenized deposits. Its phase-one reporting includes a digital voucher program.

Singapore

Purpose Bound Money

The Monetary Authority of Singapore’s Purpose Bound Money whitepaper describes how conditions can be attached to use while preserving the underlying payment instrument.

These examples motivate design comparisons. Evidence of technical operation alone leaves causal effects on access, targeting, and administrative cost open.

Implications and limits

The framework gives public agencies a decision rule for whether to enforce a condition before payment and when to retain human review. It also applies to ordinary digital payment systems that can enforce the same rules, so comparing equivalent designs helps identify whether any benefit comes from the payment architecture, better registries, or a redesigned administrative process.

The paper specifies hypotheses and evaluation methods and presents no causal estimates.

Frequently asked questions

What are programmable vouchers?

Programmable vouchers are digital benefits whose conditions, such as merchant eligibility and expiry, can be verified when a beneficiary attempts payment. In the model proposed by Jace Shim (2026), the question is not whether rules can run at checkout but when moving enforcement there improves public spending.

Do fewer improper payments mean a better voucher program?

Not necessarily. The central proposition of the model is that an apparent improvement in compliance can conceal a loss of access when a system blocks eligible purchases or leads households to give up before checkout. The paper’s hypothetical example shows a design that saves 9,000 units in harm and burden but loses 12,000 units of value from 60 fewer completed purchases.

How does the model measure whether checkout enforcement adds value?

It records three outcomes per 1,000 eligible households: eligible purchase completion (T), expected harm from noncompliant spending that succeeds (D), and total implementation and use cost (C). The change in value is w × change in T − change in D − change in C, where w is the social value of reaching an additional eligible household. A minimum completion rate for groups with access barriers is set before deployment.

Which voucher rules are suited to checkout enforcement?

Rules based on an authoritative merchant registry and reliable expiry timestamps may be straightforward to apply at checkout. Rules that depend on a merchant’s changing inventory, ambiguous item categories, or hardship circumstances can be harder to encode, and outdated records or system failures may block authorized purchases.

Should public agencies keep human review when vouchers are programmable?

The model gives agencies a decision rule for when to retain human review. A staffed override or later review can recover wrongly blocked transactions while preserving an auditable decision trail, and Proposition 2 predicts that a documented assisted path and a time-bounded override reduce the completion gap.

How do Project Hangang and Purpose Bound Money relate to the model?

The Bank of Korea describes Project Hangang as an experiment combining wholesale central bank digital currency with bank-issued tokenized deposits, with phase-one reporting that includes a digital voucher program. The Monetary Authority of Singapore’s Purpose Bound Money whitepaper describes attaching conditions to use while preserving the underlying payment instrument. The paper treats both as implementation context; evidence of technical operation alone leaves effects on access, targeting, and administrative cost open.
