Stablecoin and digital money research

Three book frameworks, fifteen conceptual models, and two design white papers developed from Dollar on Chain and Target Money: where stablecoin money goes, who controls access to it, when programmable rules improve public spending, how programmable payments separate value, purpose, and evidence, who should authorize payment for care, when freelancers should get paid, how to measure the tax of distrust, how to pay for verified carbon reductions, whether portable reputation can improve supplier finance, whether small merchants can join without an upfront deposit, when AI agents should pay per API call, when machines should settle metered usage, when automated payments should pause, how to pay for local biodiversity outcomes, and whether two tokens separate financial risk.

Frameworks from Target Money

Three frameworks are set out in the book itself (Raon Book, 2026; Korean edition 《타겟 머니》) and organize the working papers below.

The Four Vectors of Trust Architecture →

Where does payment lose its purpose, and what evidence must travel with it?

Four models that bind payment to verified evidence: agent–objective binding, milestone settlement, on-chain reputation, and exogenous value monetization. Target Money, Part 3.

Exogenous Value Monetization Model →

How can unpriced environmental and social value be verified and settled?

Vector 4: programmable eco-capital, with a monetization function that weights outcomes by evidence integrity and time. Target Money, Chapter 11.

Side-Value Catcher Tokenomics →

How can a stable payment token and a value-capturing governance token be kept apart?

A separated capital layer in which a governance token captures fee and reserve income while the payment token stays stable and yield-free. Target Money, Chapter 7.

The fifteen models

Stablecoin Reserve–Distribution Feedback Model →

When does stablecoin growth create demand for U.S. Treasury bills, and when can reserve income fund further network expansion?

Payment access, net issuance, reserve allocation, and reserve income linked in a conditional loop, with issuer bill purchases kept distinct from net bill demand. Zenodo, DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24577526.

Who Controls Stablecoin Payment Networks? →

How much of the gains from cooperation does an issuer keep when wallets, exchanges, and payment processors control customer access?

A weighted Nash bargaining model in which a distributor’s share depends on each side’s outside option and bargaining weight in a payment corridor. Zenodo, DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24578797.

When Do Programmable Vouchers Improve Public Spending? →

When does enforcing voucher rules at checkout improve public spending, and when does it cost eligible households access?

An evaluation model weighing eligible purchase completion, prevented improper spending, and cost across agencies, merchants, and households. Zenodo, DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24578261.

Programmable Money in Three Layers →

What makes a stablecoin payment programmable, and when should settlement, rules, and evidence be kept separate?

The Value–Purpose–Evidence Model: a token settles, an agreement sets the condition, and a separate evidence process decides whether it was met. Zenodo, DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24580931.

Who Should Authorize Payment for Care? →

When a care visit record is incomplete, who decides whether the provider is paid, and how much can be released now?

The Agent–Purpose Binding Model: the funder sets the purpose, the provider claims against it, a designated reviewer resolves conflicts, and the supported portion is released first. Zenodo, DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24581092.

When Should Freelancers Get Paid? →

In a cross-border contract, who finances the work while acceptance or payment is pending?

Milestone-based settlement: a price and acceptance rule for each reviewable deliverable, with unpaid-work exposure and escrow exposure measured in currency-days. Zenodo, DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24581270.

The Tax of Distrust →

What must be counted before calling a new payment control cheaper?

A comparative accounting method for the hidden costs of conditional payments: verification, unresolved losses, fees, financing delays, and exclusion, with incidence and resource accounts. Zenodo, DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24581402.

Paying for Verified Carbon Reductions →

Can faster settlement coexist with credible correction when a verified reduction is later revised?

Verified carbon settlement: payment against a mitigation record or registry event, with a holdback for corrections and controls that separate duplicate payment from double counting. Zenodo, DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24581582.

Can Portable Reputation Improve Supplier Finance? →

Can a small supplier’s verified trade history help it obtain working capital from a lender that cannot see that history?

Portable reputation: authenticated, time-bounded trade records that separate evidence of past performance from evidence that a specific invoice can be financed. Zenodo, DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24581818.

Can Small Merchants Join Without an Upfront Deposit? →

What must replace a merchant deposit so that payouts stay reliable when it is removed?

Deposit-free merchant entry: separate entry deposits, settlement liquidity, and loss resources, with a funding-gap test of max(0, P − C) at each payout cutoff. Zenodo, DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24581885.

When Should AI Agents Pay per API Call? →

Should an AI agent settle every API call, batch metered calls, or subscribe?

A payment-frequency break-even rule: separate authorization, metering, billing frequency, and settlement, and batch when (N − B) × f exceeds the added cost of collection delay and reconciliation. Zenodo, DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24591069.

When Should Machines Settle Metered Usage? →

Should electricity or compute usage be paid on provisional readings, and how are later corrections reconciled?

Metered machine-to-machine settlement: separate recording, validation, charging, and transfer, and prefer fast settlement when G exceeds F + R. Zenodo, DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24591455.

When Should Automated Payments Pause? →

When oracle evidence is uncertain, should every payment stop, or only the affected obligations?

The scoped payment hold rule: hold only obligations whose evidence is materially uncertain, and hold when q × L exceeds H, while supported amounts are paid. Zenodo, DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24611980.

Paying for Local Biodiversity Outcomes →

How can a conservation fund pay for verified habitat improvements when results from different sites are not equivalent?

Staged biodiversity outcome payments under a local contract: separate tranches for documented work, a first validated outcome, and a later persistence check. Zenodo, DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24612008.

Do Two Tokens Separate Financial Risk? →

Can a payment token keep serving redemptions after its governance token and supporting market are impaired?

The dual-token risk separation test: map shared dependencies and stress redemptions and operations at two horizons, excluding governance-token sale proceeds. Zenodo, DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24612007.

Design white papers

Two standalone design white papers condense chapters of Target Money into a proposed care payment architecture. Both are conceptual designs and record no authorization, live issuance, offering, or legal finding.

Care Cash (KRWC) →

How would a won-referenced payment instrument settle conditional care payments?

Technical and regulatory design white paper: roles, par redemption and reserve testing, care settlement safeguards, and implementation gates. Chapter 15. Zenodo, DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24612001.

Care Governance Token (GOVC) →

What would a governance token decide in the Care Cash architecture, and what must stay outside its votes?

Technical and capital-engineering design white paper: governance scope, allocation, fee discipline, and backstops under joint stress. Chapter 16. Zenodo, DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24612003.

How the models connect

The feedback model asks whether retained reserve income can finance payment network expansion. The bargaining model asks how much of that income an issuer can retain when distribution depends on a few providers. Together they explain why two stablecoins with similar issuance can differ in their market effects and in their capacity to fund expansion. The voucher model turns to public use of programmable money, asking when rules that execute during payment improve spending once access, improper payments, and administrative cost are weighed together. The three-layer model asks which system owns the rule, the evidence, and the payout for each conditional claim, and whether each can change without forcing a change in the others. The care model applies that architecture to home care, adding decision rights, a time-limited review path, and a rule for releasing the supported portion of a claim. The freelance model turns to bilateral commercial contracts and asks who effectively finances the work while acceptance or settlement is pending. The tax of distrust supplies the accounting method for evaluating all of these designs, following costs across institutions rather than within one agency’s books. Verified carbon settlement applies the same architecture to climate finance, where a result can satisfy the initial payment rule and later be revised. Portable reputation asks whether evidence from completed contracts stays useful when a supplier seeks financing elsewhere, and deposit-free merchant entry asks what must fund payouts once merchants no longer post an entry deposit. The agentic payments model applies the same separation of authority, evidence, and settlement to AI agents buying API services, and metered machine settlement extends it to quantities that can change after initial approval. The scoped payment hold asks whether to release funds at all while evidence is uncertain, biodiversity outcome payments carry the carbon logic to site-specific ecological evidence and long-term stewardship, and the two-token test checks whether a payment instrument stays redeemable when its governance token fails, the question behind the KRWC and GOVC white papers.

Source books
