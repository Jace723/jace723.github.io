Stablecoin and digital money research

Three conceptual models developed from Dollar on Chain: where stablecoin money goes, who controls access to it, and when programmable rules improve public spending.

The three models

Stablecoin Reserve–Distribution Feedback Model →

When does stablecoin growth create demand for U.S. Treasury bills, and when can reserve income fund further network expansion?

Payment access, net issuance, reserve allocation, and reserve income linked in a conditional loop, with issuer bill purchases kept distinct from net bill demand. Zenodo, DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24577526.

Who Controls Stablecoin Payment Networks? →

How much of the gains from cooperation does an issuer keep when wallets, exchanges, and payment processors control customer access?

A weighted Nash bargaining model in which a distributor’s share depends on each side’s outside option and bargaining weight in a payment corridor. Zenodo, DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24578797.

When Do Programmable Vouchers Improve Public Spending? →

When does enforcing voucher rules at checkout improve public spending, and when does it cost eligible households access?

An evaluation model weighing eligible purchase completion, prevented improper spending, and cost across agencies, merchants, and households. Working paper, Version 1.0.

How the models connect

The feedback model asks whether retained reserve income can finance payment network expansion. The bargaining model asks how much of that income an issuer can retain when distribution depends on a few providers. Together they explain why two stablecoins with similar issuance can differ in their market effects and in their capacity to fund expansion. The voucher model turns to public use of programmable money, asking when rules that execute during payment improve spending once access, improper payments, and administrative cost are weighed together.

Source book

Both models are developed from Dollar on Chain by Jace Shim (Raon Book, 2026; ISBN 979-11-998906-2-6; Korean edition 《스테이블코인 부의 기회》): chapters 5, 7, and 9 for the feedback model, chapters 9 and 10 for the bargaining model, and chapters 6 and 12 for the voucher model.
