What is deposit-free merchant entry?

Definition · Deposit-Free Merchant EntryDeposit-free merchant entry, as developed by Jace Shim (2026), lets merchants join a payment network without locking up funds before their first sale. The model holds that lower upfront costs can improve entry where cash constraints are binding, provided a committed liquidity facility covers settlement timing gaps and a separate allocation of losses covers merchant default.

The starting pointA payment network may require a merchant to lock up funds before its first sale.

Why remove the deposit?

A small repair shop considering a new payment network may have little idea how many customers will use it. A refundable deposit still ties up money that could pay for parts, wages, or rent, and the cost can be especially high relative to expected sales for merchants testing a second payment channel.

Requiring a volatile governance token as the deposit adds price exposure even before the merchant makes a sale. Target Money proposes entry without such a token purchase; the paper examines the economic conditions under which dropping an upfront deposit can work.

Three balances that a deposit can blur

Merchant entry depositPaid to get inMoney the participant locks to gain access.
Settlement liquidityPaid out on timeMoney available when payouts fall due.
Loss resourcesAbsorbs failuresCovers shortfalls after a merchant or counterparty fails to meet an obligation.

A deposit sometimes supports one of the latter two functions, depending on its terms. Once the deposit is removed, the operator must identify which function needs replacement.

A test of liquidity and loss capacity

PValue of obligations due at a payout cutoff
CUsable, unencumbered cash and confirmed inflows available by that cutoff

Funding gap = max(0, P − C)

The operator can cover the gap with its own liquid assets or a committed facility that can actually be drawn in time, tracking available funding by currency and payout window. The CPMI’s work on cross-border payment liquidity concerns a different institutional setting, but illustrates why the location and availability of funding can affect payment costs.

Liquidity

A liquidity facility provides funding for a timing gap and typically creates a repayment obligation.

Losses

Refunds, reversals, operational errors, or failures to collect: calculate gross exposure and expected recoveries separately, assign the residual to the contractually responsible party, and verify it can bear it.

What does not countAn internal credit entry or an estimate based on token prices cannot by itself fund a payout or absorb a loss.

Applying the model to a merchant pilot

StepWhat the operator does
1 · AdmitLet a group of participating merchants join without a fixed deposit
2 · Disclose payout termsOffer the standard payout schedule and disclose a capped early-payout option where the operator can fund it
3 · Assign losses firstAssign responsibility for a failed collection or refund before the first transaction
4 · ReconcileRecord each payout against the underlying purchase and the funds available to settle it
5 · Fall backIf available liquidity is insufficient, apply the disclosed standard schedule or cap new early payouts
6 · SegregateDistinguish funds held for stablecoin redemption from funds committed to merchant advances

Where stablecoins fit

A stablecoin may carry approved payouts. Track the time until proceeds are usable, including conversion and withdrawal where relevant, and compare the same deposit and payout policies on conventional rails. The trial does not require a governance token or an internal claim on a thinly traded asset.

How to evaluate whether entry actually improves

Compare an existing refundable deposit with deposit-free entry at comparable fee and payout terms. If fees or payout limits must change, record those changes and separately estimate their effect where design permits. A phased or randomized offer can support evaluation where feasible.

MeasureWhy
Registration, first completed sale, and sustained useDistinguishes initial sign-ups from active use
Provider working-capital costsCaptures what the operator pays to fund early payouts
Payout failures and realized lossesTests settlement reliability without a deposit
Movement to slower payout schedulesShows whether the benefit is being clawed back through payout terms
Merchants that decline the offerAvoids measuring only those who opted in
H1

Adoption hypothesis

Deposit-free entry should produce a larger response among merchants with limited cash buffers and uncertain sales, provided their ongoing charges and access to payouts remain acceptable.

H2

Risk hypothesis

Lower deposits can coexist with reliable settlement when funding covers stressed timing gaps and loss-bearing arrangements remain credible.

Report results by merchant size, and compare whether added fees or payout delays offset the benefit to small merchants.

The paper reports no empirical estimates.

Why do payment networks ask merchants for deposits?

How do you calculate a settlement funding gap?

At each payout cutoff, let P be the value of obligations due and C the usable, unencumbered cash and confirmed inflows available by that cutoff. The funding gap is the greater of zero and P − C. An operator can cover it with its own liquid assets or a committed facility that can actually be drawn in time, tracked by currency and payout window.

Is a liquidity facility the same as loss protection?

No. A liquidity facility provides funding for a timing gap and typically creates a repayment obligation. Losses from refunds, reversals, operational errors, or failures to collect need a separate allocation to a party with resources to bear them. In the paper’s example, if 2,000 of expected funds prove irrecoverable, the operator still owes the facility provider; the agreement determines who bears the loss.
