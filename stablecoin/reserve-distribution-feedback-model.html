The Stablecoin Reserve–Distribution Feedback Model

How dollar payment networks transmit demand to the U.S. Treasury bill market

What is the Stablecoin Reserve–Distribution Feedback Model?

DefinitionThe Stablecoin Reserve–Distribution Feedback Model is a conceptual model, proposed by Jace Shim (2026) and developed from Dollar on Chain, of how dollar-backed stablecoins link payment networks to short-term sovereign debt markets through the balance sheets of private issuers. Wider distribution can increase demand to hold stablecoins; when that demand leads to net issuance, issuers allocate the proceeds to reserves, including Treasury bills where permitted; and reserve income can then support integrations and distribution, subject to margins and issuer strategy.

Research question

Under what conditions does the expansion of dollar stablecoin payment networks create incremental demand for U.S. Treasury bills, and when can reserve income support further network expansion?

The feedback loop in four steps

  1. Payment accessNew merchant integrations, payment processors, and settlement corridors expand the ways users can spend or receive a stablecoin.
  2. Working balances and net issuanceIf participants consequently hold larger working balances, net issuance can rise.
  3. Reserve allocationIssuers allocate the proceeds of net issuance to reserves, including Treasury bills where permitted.
  4. Reserve income to distributionPart of an increase in retained reserve income can fund integrations, such as settlement infrastructure or onboarding additional payment partners.

The loop then returns to step 1: new integrations attract larger maintained balances, which can raise net issuance again. Each link is conditional, and the model specifies when it can weaken or reverse.

Two accounting relationships

1. Change in the issuer’s Treasury bill holdings

Let St be outstanding stablecoins at face value and Bt the issuer’s direct Treasury bill holdings, with bt = Bt / St for St > 0. The ratio bt combines reserve coverage and bill allocation.

ΔBt = bt−1ΔSt + St−1Δbt + ΔStΔbt

This stock identity separates changes in issuance from changes in reserve composition and their interaction.

2. From issuer purchases to net demand

Let QtI be the issuer’s net bill purchases attributable to an issuance shock, and QtO the reduction in net bill purchases by other holders attributable to the same shock, each measured against a counterfactual without the shock.

Qtnet = QtI − QtO

This expression tracks the demand shift before further equilibrium adjustment. The model makes no assumption that every dollar of issuance creates an equal dollar of incremental bill demand.

Networks with similar issuance growth can therefore have different market effects because of funding sources and portfolio choices.

Relationship to existing research

What it builds on

Ahmed and Aldasoro’s BIS working paper estimates the effects of stablecoin flows on short-term Treasury yields. The OECD Global Debt Report 2026 discusses the prospect of additional bill demand and the possibility that issuer holdings crowd out existing demand.

What it adds

It connects the funding source and reserve allocation to a distinct issuer decision: whether to spend retained reserve income on expanding distribution. Its contribution is a testable specification of that feedback and the conditions under which it operates.

Three testable propositions

The propositions are falsifiable claims to be tested; the paper supplies no statistical estimates.

Proposition 1 — Payment access and maintained balances

Comparable payment integrations should be followed by larger increases in net issuance in markets where users and intermediaries maintain working balances. Where tokens circulate faster and working balances stay flat, transaction growth should have a weaker relationship with issuance.

Proposition 2 — Funding-source offset

For comparable issuance shocks and issuer bill allocations, initial net bill demand should increase less when the issuance displaces bill-heavy investment products. Where the displaced holdings exceed the issuer’s purchases, the net demand shift should be negative.

Proposition 3 — Retained income and distribution

Higher retained reserve income should precede greater distribution investment among issuers that finance expansion internally, conditional on initial scale, market demand, liquidity needs and distribution contracts. The relationship should weaken where intermediaries capture most of the marginal income or where distribution is already saturated.

Empirical research design

Use issuer-by-month observations for issuance, reserve composition and observable distribution expenditure. Track net issuance across chains while excluding bridge transfers and tokens held in issuer treasury accounts. Measure adoption through dated partner launches, payment-processor settlement data or merchant acceptance; trading volumes and blockchain address counts alone are weak measures.

Scope and limitations

In scope

Redeemable dollar tokens issued against identifiable reserves. Direct Treasury bill holdings are the main outcome; indirect holdings through money funds or repo require separate measurement.

Claims the model does not make

It does not assume that every dollar of issuance creates an equal dollar of bill demand, and it does not provide statistical estimates. Inferring durable monetary influence from this route would require additional evidence on sustained use across markets and the availability of competing payment rails.

Frequently asked questions

What is the Stablecoin Reserve–Distribution Feedback Model?

The Stablecoin Reserve–Distribution Feedback Model is a conceptual model, proposed by Jace Shim (2026) and developed from Dollar on Chain, of how dollar-backed stablecoins link payment networks to short-term sovereign debt markets through the balance sheets of private issuers. Wider distribution can increase demand to hold stablecoins; when that demand leads to net issuance, issuers allocate the proceeds to reserves, including Treasury bills where permitted; and reserve income can then support integrations and distribution, subject to margins and issuer strategy.

Does stablecoin growth automatically increase demand for U.S. Treasury bills?

No. The model makes no assumption that every dollar of issuance creates an equal dollar of incremental bill demand. Net demand equals the issuer’s bill purchases minus the reduction in other holders’ purchases caused by the same issuance shock. If new stablecoins are bought by redeeming a bill-heavy money market fund, issuer purchases may be partly or fully offset.

How can stablecoin reserve income fund the expansion of dollar payments?

Issuers earn income on reserves, including Treasury bills. The feedback closes if an issuer allocates part of an increase in retained income to integrations, such as settlement infrastructure or new payment partners, and those integrations lead users and intermediaries to hold larger balances. Low margins, weak demand for working balances, or costly distributor agreements can interrupt it.

What happens to the feedback when stablecoins are redeemed?

Redemptions shrink issuance outstanding. Issuers able to meet them with cash or maturing bills may need few outright bill sales. Sales become more plausible when redemption needs exceed immediately available liquidity and funding capacity; redemption speed, maturity profile and market conditions shape how quickly the channel reverses.

How does this model differ from existing research on stablecoins and Treasury yields?

Ahmed and Aldasoro (BIS Working Paper No. 1270) estimate the effects of stablecoin flows on short-term Treasury yields, and the OECD Global Debt Report 2026 discusses additional bill demand and possible crowding out. This model connects the funding source and reserve allocation to a distinct issuer decision: whether to spend retained reserve income on expanding distribution. Its contribution is a testable specification of that feedback and its conditions.
