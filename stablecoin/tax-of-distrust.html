What is the tax of distrust?

Definition · Tax of DistrustThe tax of distrust, as developed by Jace Shim (2026), is the measurable burden of verifying, enforcing, and settling an exchange when participants cannot take eligibility, performance, or payment for granted. It is measured against a specified comparison and over a fixed population and period, and includes resources spent on routine checks, losses and delays left unresolved by those checks, and the burden imposed on legitimate participants.

Why it mattersA payment system can report lower fraud and smaller audit budgets while leaving merchants and recipients with more paperwork, more rejections, and longer waits.

The cost that disappears from the agency’s books

A local authority pays suppliers for eligible equipment. Its staff used to audit a sample of invoices after payment. Under a new system, every invoice is checked before money moves.

What the agency’s dashboard shows

  • The audit budget falls
  • Fewer payments are flagged after settlement

What can be missing from it

  • Small suppliers spend more time correcting records
  • A handful give up on valid claims

Williamson (1979) treats governance as a response to the costs of contracting and adapting under uncertainty. Target Money applies that insight to digital payments, where convenience at the moment of transfer can coexist with considerable work before and after it. The paper asks: what needs to be counted before calling a new control cheaper?

Recording the last two explicitly means that a system that reduces fraud by refusing more claims can be assessed on both outcomes.

Two accounts for the same program

Incidence account · for each actor

Payer, recipient or supplier, payment provider, and any third-party reviewer

  • Staff and user time
  • Unrecovered losses
  • Net fees paid or received
  • Cost of delayed access to funds
  • Location of any unserved eligible transaction

Keep cash outlays and estimates separate. A payment reversed after review contributes its residual loss, recovery effort, and delay only once.

Resource account · for the program as a whole

  • Labor, infrastructure, mediation, and actual payment-processing resources, counted once
  • Fees between included actors treated as transfers
  • Gross misdirection and recoveries recorded as separate public-budget measures
  • Any social loss from defeated program purpose, excluding principal already counted as a transfer

If a provider’s processing cost is unknown, report a range or leave it unestimated; a fee is a useful private cost measure and only a rough proxy for resources consumed.

Measuring delay

Use the incremental balance and rate for the party that actually loses access, and avoid adding the same financing loss to another category. Report delays in days as well, since an estimated funding cost can miss consequences such as missed payroll or temporary supplier exit.

Comparing payment rails and control design

ComparisonHold fixedWhat it estimates
Payment railAn identical authorization policyWhat stablecoin settlement changes: a possibly shorter transfer leg against added conversion, custody, onboarding, and reconciliation steps
Control designThe same payment railWhat changes when the control moves, comparing prepayment checks with postpayment review

Measure time until funds are usable. Use district or program rollout comparisons where feasible, and inspect case mix, supplier selection, and pre-existing trends. Collect timestamps, itemized operating costs, sampled independent reviews, appeals, and follow-up with noncompleters, and apply the same audit standard in both systems; otherwise a lower count of detected fraud could reflect changes in detection. Set a common observation window, follow unresolved claims through a stated cutoff, test sensitivity to recovery rates, wages, financing rates, and the value of missed deliveries, and report uncertainty and cost incidence alongside the aggregate.

Frequently asked questions

What is the tax of distrust?

The tax of distrust, as developed by Jace Shim (2026), is the measurable burden of verifying, enforcing, and settling an exchange when participants cannot take eligibility, performance, or payment for granted. It is measured against a specified comparison and over a fixed population and period, and includes resources spent on routine checks, losses and delays left unresolved by those checks, and the burden imposed on legitimate participants.

Why can a new payment control look cheaper than it is?

An agency that moves checks to the point of authorization may see its audit budget fall and fewer payments flagged after settlement, while small suppliers spend more time correcting records and some give up on valid claims. Those costs can be missing from the same dashboard that records the savings. The tax of distrust counts both.

What costs does the tax of distrust include?

Audit costs, fraud and leakage, intermediation charges, and the cost of delayed settlement, plus two items the paper records explicitly: the work of resolving exceptions and the value lost when an eligible exchange never takes place.

What is the difference between the incidence account and the resource account?

The incidence account records, for each actor, staff and user time, unrecovered losses, net fees, the cost of delayed access to funds, and unserved eligible transactions. The resource account counts the program’s labor, infrastructure, mediation, and payment-processing resources once, treating fees between included actors as transfers.

How is the cost of payment delay measured?

Sum the daily unavailable balances to get delay exposure in currency-days. A claim of 10,000 units that becomes usable ten days after approval has 100,000 currency-days of exposure, about 33 units of financing cost at a 12% annual marginal funding rate on a 365-day basis. Delays should also be reported in days, since a funding-cost estimate can miss consequences such as missed payroll.

Do stablecoins reduce the tax of distrust?

Not automatically. A stablecoin can carry an authorized payment and may shorten the transfer leg, while adding conversion, custody, onboarding, and reconciliation steps. The paper assesses stablecoin settlement as a distinct change to the payment rail, compared under an identical authorization policy.
